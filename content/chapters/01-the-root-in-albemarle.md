The roots of the family line anchor in the red clay of Albemarle County, Virginia. Before emancipation, grand-matriarch Polly had no legal surname. As an enslaved woman, she was recorded in public schedules simply as "Polly," appearing in the estate records and 1850/1860 slave property schedules of Valentine Wood Southall.

Following emancipation, Polly and her husband David gained the surname Smith, which first formally appears in the 1870 U.S. Federal Census for their Fredericksville Parish homestead. While later vital records—such as her son Isaac’s 1927 death certificate—list her maiden name as "Polly Southall," this was the name of the family that had enslaved her, serving as an archival anchor on later documents.

On October 18, 1866, Charles "Charlie" Smith was born in Albemarle County; his birth registration recorded Asher W. Southall (Valentine's son) as informant and employer. Even after official emancipation in Albemarle County Courthouse Deed Book #61 on March 23, 1865, Polly’s household remained laboring on the Southall plantation lands while building capital.

In 1889, Polly took an extraordinary step: she purchased a 23-to-29-acre commercial apple orchard plot near Earlysville in her own name out of the sprawling, insolvent Chancery lawsuit Brown’s Administrator v. Burnley. The court-appointed administrators were the Duke Law Firm. Facing intense pressure to liquidate Burnley lands, the Dukes aggressively harassed Polly for cash payments. Despite physical receipts in Aunt Marilyn's brown leather suitcase proving Polly made regular payments, the firm repeatedly served sheriff summonses on her. Holding title solely in her name was Polly's defensive shield—protecting family equity from predatory tax defaults and forced labor contracts.
